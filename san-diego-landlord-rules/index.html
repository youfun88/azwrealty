San Diego Landlord Rules for Rental Owners

Rent increases, ending a tenancy, relocation payments, deposits, and required notices, with the official sources linked.

Updated November 2, 2026

Short answer

What rules apply to landlords in San Diego?

Most San Diego rentals fall under the California rent cap of 5% plus the change in the cost of living, or 10%, whichever is lower. In the City of San Diego, a covered tenancy can end only for just cause, and a no-fault termination requires two or three months of rent in relocation assistance. Security deposits are generally limited to one month’s rent. Exemptions exist, so check your own property.

Rent increases · Ending a tenancy · Deposits · Sources

This guide is general information for rental owners, not legal advice. The city rules below come from the San Diego Municipal Code, which applies in the City of San Diego. If your rental is in another city in the county, state law still applies, and you should check whether that city has its own rules. For a specific notice or dispute, talk to a landlord-tenant attorney.

How much can I raise the rent on a San Diego rental?

For covered rentals, California caps gross rent increases in any 12-month period at 5% plus the change in the cost of living, or 10%, whichever is lower. No more than two increases are allowed in that period (Civil Code § 1947.12, as summarized in the DRE 2026 guide). You can set the starting rent at any amount, and the cap applies to increases after that. The law expires on January 1, 2030, unless extended.

The cap does not apply to these rentals, among others:

  • Housing less than 15 years old.
  • A duplex where the owner lived in one unit when the tenancy began and still lives there.
  • A single-family home or condo whose owner is not a real estate investment trust, a corporation, or an LLC with a corporate member, if the owner gave the tenant written notice that the unit is exempt.

On a month-to-month rental, a covered property needs 30 days’ written notice of an increase. An exempt property with no local rent limit needs 30 days’ notice for an increase of 10% or less and 90 days’ notice for more than 10%.

Can I end a tenancy in the City of San Diego without a reason?

Not on a covered rental. San Diego Municipal Code § 98.0704 says a landlord shall not terminate a tenancy without just cause. The city’s definition of a tenancy covers a right to occupy for more than 30 days, and leaves out fixed-term leases of three months or less. Under state law alone, just cause generally starts after a tenant has been in place for 12 months (Civil Code § 1946.2).

The two kinds of just cause in the city ordinance (summary)
At-fault examplesNo-fault reasons
Unpaid rent; a material lease breach; nuisance or waste; criminal activity on the property; refusing a substantially similar lease renewal; refusing lawful entry; unauthorized subletOwner or close family moving in; taking the rental off the market; a government or court order to vacate; demolition or a substantial remodel

A curable lease violation needs a written notice and a chance to fix it first. If it is not fixed in time, the landlord can serve a three-day notice to quit (§ 98.0706(a)).

Common exemptions from the city ordinance (§ 98.0703) include housing that received its certificate of occupancy within the previous 15 years, a two-unit building where the landlord lived in one unit at the start of the tenancy and still does, and a home that can be sold separately from any other unit when the owner is not a real estate investment trust, a corporation, or an LLC with a corporate member. That last exemption requires the exact statement from the code, and for a tenancy started or renewed on or after January 1, 2024, the statement must be in the lease. An exemption from the city ordinance does not by itself settle state law, which has its own exemption rules and notice wording.

How much relocation assistance does a San Diego landlord owe?

For a no-fault termination in the City of San Diego, § 98.0706(c) requires relocation assistance regardless of the tenant’s income or length of tenancy:

  • Two months of the tenant’s actual rent.
  • Three months if the tenant is 62 or older or disabled.
  • You choose a direct payment within 15 days of the notice, or a waiver of the same amount of rent.
  • It is in addition to returning any deposit owed to the tenant.

State law alone requires one month’s rent, paid or waived. The written notice must describe the reason and state the tenant’s right to relocation assistance. For some reasons, including owner move-in and remodel, it must also state the tenant’s right to a first offer if the home is offered for rent again within five years and the tenant asked for this in writing.

Mistakes are costly. Failing to strictly follow § 98.0706 voids the termination notice, and a landlord who fails to pay required relocation assistance is liable for at least three times that amount plus actual economic damages (§ 98.0709).

Can I move back into my San Diego home if I rent it out?

This matters if you are renting out your home after moving away. Under § 98.0704(b)(1), owner move-in is a no-fault reason, with conditions:

  • For leases entered into on or after June 24, 2023, it applies only if the tenant agrees in writing, or the lease expressly lets the owner end the lease to move in.
  • The occupant must be the owner or the owner’s spouse, domestic partner, child, grandchild, parent, or grandparent.
  • That person must move in within 90 days after the tenant leaves and live there at least 12 continuous months as a primary residence.
  • If that does not happen, the owner must offer the home back to the tenant at the same rent and terms, and pay reasonable moving costs beyond any relocation assistance already paid.

If you may want the home back, raise this before you sign the lease, not after.

What notices do I have to give tenants in San Diego?

  • City tenant protections notice: covered rentals need the notice in § 98.0705, in at least 12-point type, plus the San Diego Housing Commission’s Tenant Protection Guide. For a tenancy started or renewed on or after June 24, 2023, both go in the lease or in a written notice the tenant signs at lease signing.
  • Termination notice: no-fault terminations need at least 30 or 60 days’ written notice under Civil Code § 1946.1. It is generally 60 days if every tenant has lived there a year or more, and may be 30 days if any tenant has lived there less than a year.
  • Housing Commission notice: the code also calls for written notice to the Commission within three business days of a termination notice. This duty starts 30 days after the Commission opens its submission portal and gives public notice of it, so check whether that has happened.
  • Lead paint: for homes built before 1978, disclose known lead-based paint and hazards, give the federal pamphlet, and include the lead warning statement in the rental agreement.
  • Bed bugs: give the required written notice before a new tenancy begins.

Since January 1, 2026, a rental must also have a working stove and refrigerator to count as habitable, unless the landlord and tenant agree the tenant will provide them (AB 628, listed in the DRE 2026 guide).

How large can a security deposit be in California?

Generally no more than one month’s rent, furnished or unfurnished (Civil Code § 1950.5). Pet deposits, key fees, and cleaning fees all count toward that limit. Small landlords, meaning individuals or LLCs owned by people that own and rent one or two properties with no more than 4 rental units, can charge up to two months’ rent, but only one month’s rent to a service member.

The DRE 2026 guide also lists these duties:

  • Move-in photos: for tenancies beginning on or after July 1, 2025, photograph the unit immediately before or at the beginning of the tenancy.
  • Move-out photos: photograph the unit after the tenant returns it and before repairs or cleaning you plan to deduct, and again after that work is done.
  • 21 days: after move-out, refund the deposit in full or send an itemized statement of deductions with any remaining refund.
  • Digital refunds: since January 1, 2026, a deposit or rent paid digitally means the deposit generally goes back digitally, unless you both agree otherwise in writing.
