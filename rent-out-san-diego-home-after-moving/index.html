Renting Out Your San Diego Home After You Move

Getting the home back, city tax, federal and California tax points, and checking a manager, with the official sources linked.

Updated November 2, 2026

Short answer

What should I do before renting out my San Diego home after I move?

Decide whether you may want the home back, because the lease you sign affects that. Then check your loan, insurance and any HOA rules, plan for the city’s Rental Unit Business Tax, ask a tax professional about depreciation and a future sale, and verify any property manager’s license with the California Department of Real Estate.

Getting it back · City tax · Federal tax · Sources

This guide is general information for homeowners, not legal or tax advice. The city rules apply in the City of San Diego. If your home is in another city in the county, state and federal rules still apply, and you should check that city’s own rules. This guide does not cover owners moving outside the United States, so ask a tax professional about that.

Can I get my San Diego home back after I rent it out?

Sometimes, and it depends on the lease you sign. Under San Diego Municipal Code § 98.0704, a landlord of a covered rental needs just cause to end a tenancy. Owner move-in is a no-fault reason, with conditions:

  • For leases entered into on or after June 24, 2023, it applies only if the tenant agrees in writing, or the lease expressly lets the owner end the lease to move in.
  • The occupant must be the owner or the owner’s spouse, domestic partner, child, grandchild, parent, or grandparent.
  • That person must move in within 90 days after the tenant leaves and live there at least 12 continuous months as a primary residence.
  • Relocation assistance applies: two months of the tenant’s actual rent, or three months if the tenant is 62 or older or disabled (§ 98.0706(c)).

Some homes are exempt from the city ordinance, including housing that received its certificate of occupancy within the previous 15 years, and a home that can be sold separately when the owner is not a real estate investment trust, a corporation, or an LLC with a corporate member. That second exemption requires the exact statement from the code, and for a tenancy started or renewed on or after January 1, 2024, the statement must be in the lease (§ 98.0703). State law has its own exemption rules and notice wording.

If you may want the home back, raise it before you sign the lease, not after. See the San Diego landlord rules guide for just cause, relocation assistance, and required notices.

What should I check before I list the home for rent?

  • Your loan: ask your lender whether your mortgage has occupancy terms that affect renting.
  • Your insurance: tell your insurer the home will be a rental and ask what coverage you need.
  • Your HOA: if the home is a condo or in an HOA, read the governing documents for rental rules.
  • Your deposit limit: a security deposit is generally limited to one month’s rent, or two months for small landlords who meet the state definition, but only one month for a service member (Civil Code § 1950.5, as summarized in the DRE 2026 guide).
  • Your move-in record: for tenancies beginning on or after July 1, 2025, photograph the unit immediately before or at the beginning of the tenancy (DRE 2026 guide).

Do I owe a City of San Diego tax on a rented home?

Yes, unless an exemption applies. The Rental Unit Business Tax is imposed annually on anyone who owns, operates, or manages the rental of residential real estate, including single-family homes. According to the City Treasurer’s FAQ:

  • It is generally due March 1 for existing owners. New owners have 30 days after the billing statement is mailed.
  • It is not prorated.
  • Not receiving a bill does not remove the duty to pay. If you have no bill by February 1, contact the Treasurer’s Office at (619) 615-1545 or rtax@sandiego.gov.
  • Owners must keep their mailing address current, so report your new address the same year you move.

How does renting out my former home affect my federal taxes?

These points come from IRS publications for 2025 returns. Check the current edition, and ask a tax professional how they apply to you.

  • Reporting: you can generally use Schedule E (Form 1040) to report rental income and expenses (IRS Topic 414).
  • Expenses in the first year: if you convert the home partway through the year, you divide yearly expenses such as taxes and insurance between rental and personal use. The IRS example: move out in May, start renting June 1, and deduct seven-twelfths of those yearly expenses as rental expenses (Publication 527).
  • Depreciation: the basis is the lesser of the home’s fair market value or your adjusted basis on the date you change it to rental use. Land is not depreciable. Residential rental property is depreciated over 27.5 years (Publication 527).

Can I still get the home sale tax exclusion if I rent out my home first?

Possibly. IRS Publication 523 sets a maximum exclusion of $250,000, or $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. The test looks for at least 24 months of ownership and 24 months of use as your residence in the 5 years before the sale.

  • The window moves: the 5 years are counted back from the sale date, so a long rental period can leave you short of the 24 months of use.
  • Time after you move out: any part of the 5-year period after you last used the home as your main home is not nonqualified use.
  • Depreciation: you cannot exclude the part of your gain equal to depreciation allowed or allowable for periods after May 6, 1997.

If a sale is likely within a few years, talk to a tax professional before you sign a long lease.

How do I check a property manager before I move away?

The DRE’s quick guide for landlords gives a practical list:

  • License: it must be valid and active to collect rent and manage your property. Check at dre.ca.gov that the status is “Licensed”, and that a salesperson works for a broker.
  • Agreement: make sure it explains extra fees and how repairs are handled, requires monthly accounting reports, and has a clear termination date.
  • Money: make sure the manager has a trust account for deposits and rents, unless they are held in escrow or given directly to you.
  • Proof: be alert if the manager does not send copies of invoices, or does not disclose all income sources from managing your property.
