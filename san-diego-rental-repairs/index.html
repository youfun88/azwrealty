Who Pays for Repairs on a San Diego Rental?

What landlords must fix, what tenants must do, notice before entering, and what happens if repairs are not made, with the official source linked.

Updated November 2, 2026

Short answer

What repairs must a landlord make on a San Diego rental?

Under California law, the landlord must make the home fit to live in before renting it and repair problems that make it unfit during the tenancy. The landlord does not have to fix damage caused by the tenant or the tenant’s family, guests, or pets. Less serious repairs are usually decided by the rental agreement.

Habitable homes · Tenant duties · Entering · If repairs are not made · Sources

This guide is general information for rental owners, not legal advice. These are state rules, summarized from the DRE 2026 guide, which is written for tenants. Local building and health codes also apply. For a specific dispute, talk to a landlord-tenant attorney.

What makes a California rental habitable?

A rental must be fit for people to live in and substantially comply with state and local building and health codes that materially affect tenants’ health and safety. A home may be considered uninhabitable if it substantially lacks any of these:

  • Waterproofing and weather protection of the roof and exterior walls, including unbroken windows and doors.
  • Plumbing in good working order, with hot and cold running water connected to a sewage disposal system.
  • Gas, heating, and electrical systems in good working order.
  • Clean and sanitary buildings and grounds, free from debris, rodents, and vermin at the start of the tenancy, and in areas within the landlord’s control after that.
  • Adequate trash receptacles in good repair.
  • Floors, stairways, and railings in good repair.

Each unit must also have a working toilet, wash basin, and bathtub or shower, natural light, and safe exits, as well as:

  • Locks: operable dead bolts on main entry doors, and operable locks or security devices on windows.
  • Detectors: working smoke detectors, and working carbon monoxide detectors if the home has fossil-fuel appliances or an attached garage.
  • Stove and refrigerator: since January 1, 2026, both must be in good working order, unless you and the tenant agree the tenant will provide their own.

Visible mold growth that a health or code enforcement officer judges to be more than superficial may also be a substandard condition. Minor code violations and cosmetic flaws do not by themselves make a home uninhabitable, as long as they do not affect habitability.

What repairs is the tenant responsible for?

The landlord is not responsible under the implied warranty of habitability for damage caused by the tenant or the tenant’s family, guests, or pets. The law also requires tenants to:

  • Keep the home as clean and sanitary as its condition permits, and dispose of trash properly.
  • Use gas, electrical, and plumbing fixtures properly.
  • Not damage, deface, or remove any part of the home, or let anyone else do so.
  • Use the rooms for their intended purposes.
  • Tell the landlord when dead bolts, window locks, or smoke or carbon monoxide detectors do not work.

A landlord does not have to repair a condition if the tenant’s failure to do these things substantially caused it, or substantially interfered with the repair. A minor violation by the tenant does not remove the landlord’s duty.

Can a lease make the tenant responsible for repairs?

Partly. You and a tenant may agree that the tenant does some repairs and maintenance in exchange for lower rent. The agreement must be made in good faith, with a real reduction in rent and a tenant who intends to and can do the work. Regardless of any such agreement, the landlord is ultimately responsible for maintaining the property as state and local housing codes require.

For less serious items, such as a washing machine, parking, or a pool, the rental agreement usually decides who makes the repair. A landlord cannot change the agreement alone to shift habitability repairs to the tenant after move-in.

How much notice must a landlord give before entering for repairs?

Reasonable advance written notice, stating the date, approximate time, and purpose of the entry. Entry is generally limited to normal business hours, 8 a.m. to 5 p.m., seven days a week. The law considers 24 hours’ written notice reasonable in most situations, and mailing at least six days ahead reasonable if the notice is mailed.

Advance written notice is not required in these cases:

  • An emergency.
  • The tenant has moved out or abandoned the home.
  • The tenant is present and consents at the time of entry.
  • You and the tenant orally agreed on the repair, with a date and approximate time within one week of that agreement.

What can a tenant do if the landlord does not make repairs?

The DRE guide describes several remedies, including repair and deduct, rent withholding, and abandonment, each with its own requirements. For repair and deduct:

  • The defect must be serious and directly related to health and safety, such as a roof leak in the rainy season, no hot running water, or a gas leak.
  • The repair cannot cost more than one month’s rent.
  • The tenant cannot use this remedy more than twice in any 12-month period.
  • The tenant or the tenant’s family, guests, or pets must not have caused the defect.
  • The tenant must tell the landlord and allow a reasonable time. The law usually considers 30 days reasonable, but it can be shorter. For a broken furnace in very cold weather, one to two days may be reasonable.

The law prohibits retaliation, such as ending a tenancy, raising the rent, or decreasing services because a tenant used this remedy. Respond to repair requests promptly, and keep a dated record of each request and repair.
